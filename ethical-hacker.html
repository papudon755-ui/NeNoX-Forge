Ethical Hacker / Security Specialist

Role 01 • Cybersecurity & Infrastructure

Professional toolkit: Linux, Bash, Nmap, Metasploit, Wireshark, Burp Suite, Network Security

Ethical hacking is the authorized and methodical practice of evaluating computers, applications, networks, cloud environments, and connected services to identify security weaknesses before those weaknesses are discovered or abused by malicious attackers. An ethical hacker operates with written permission, a defined scope, rules of engagement, and a clear responsibility to protect systems and sensitive information.

The role combines offensive creativity with defensive discipline: the specialist thinks like an attacker, validates realistic attack paths in a controlled manner, measures business impact, and helps engineering teams reduce risk.

Professional work may include reconnaissance, asset discovery, vulnerability assessment, web and API testing, authentication review, privilege-boundary analysis, configuration auditing, cloud-security checks, incident support, and evidence-based reporting.

Ethical hacking is not simply running tools; it is producing reliable findings, explaining them clearly, prioritizing remediation, and verifying fixes. All testing must happen only on systems with explicit authorization.

Tools and key capabilities

  • Nmap — discovers hosts, scans ports, identifies services and maps the approved network attack surface.
  • Metasploit — validates known vulnerabilities in controlled labs and authorized assessments without unnecessary damage.
  • Wireshark — captures and analyzes packets to investigate protocols, suspicious traffic and insecure communication.
  • Burp Suite — tests web applications and APIs by inspecting requests, sessions, input validation and access controls.
  • Linux and Bash — provide a flexible environment for investigation, automation, scripting and repeatable workflows.
  • Nikto and OpenVAS — support authorized web-server and vulnerability assessment activities.
  • SIEM and log platforms — correlate events, identify unusual behavior and support incident investigation.
  • Threat-modeling frameworks — help identify assets, trust boundaries, attack paths and practical mitigations.
